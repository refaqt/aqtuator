Damper Sizing

A linear motor stage runs away from one end at full force and hits the end stop. The page finds the impact speed from the speed limit of the drive or the motor's own top speed. Then it calculates one of two kinds of end stop: an oil damper (shock absorber) with the force law F = c·vⁿ, or a short polyurethane bumper whose force depends on how far it is pressed in. Choose the kind under "Type of end stop". The page shows how the energy goes into the end stop along its stroke.

The damper force is highest at the moment of impact and falls with the speed. A catalog shock absorber with several orifices that close along the stroke keeps the force close to even. The dashed line shows that ideal case: the same energy, spread evenly over the stroke. It gives the lowest peak force a stroke can have.

The bumper force starts at zero (or, for the constant curve, at its full value) and is highest where the carriage stops. So the peak force sits at the end, the opposite of an oil damper. The ACE TA curve rises fast in the first few millimetres and then flattens, so it comes closer to an even force than a linear spring. The dashed line is the even force over the full bumper stroke, the lowest peak force this stroke can have. A short stroke always means a high force.

How this is calculated

Speed-up. The carriage starts at rest. The motor gives its maximum force until its back voltage uses up the drive voltage. Per phase: (E + R/2·I)² + (ω·L/2·I)² ≤ (η·V_dc/√6)², with E = Ke/√3·v and ω = 2π·v/τ. The force is k_f·I with the largest current that meets this and the current limit. The page steps m·dv/dt = F(v) in time until the carriage has moved the acceleration stroke. If the drive has a speed limit, the carriage speeds up to it and then rolls on at that speed.

Damper. Along the damper stroke x: m·v·dv/dx = F_motor·(push) − c·vⁿ. The page finds c by bisection so that the speed at the end of the stroke is the allowed end speed. Energy balance: ½·m·v₀² + W_motor = W_damper + ½·m·v_end². The page shows how far off it is.

Bumper. The bumper force depends on the compression x only: F = F_full·g(x/s). The page sets F_full so that the work at the full stroke s is the catalog energy E. The shape g is one of: the ACE TA curve g(u) = 1 − (1 − u)^1.8, fitted to the ACE force–stroke graph of the TA37-16; a constant force g = 1; a linear spring g = u; or a power law g = uᵖ. The page steps m·dv/dt = F_motor·(push) − F(x) in time until the carriage stops or the bumper is fully pressed in. Then metal hits metal, and the energy that is left has no stroke to go into. Of the bumper work, the part "turned into heat" stays in the bumper. The rest pushes the carriage back: ½·m·v_back² = (1 − absorbed)·W_bumper. Real elastomers get stiffer at high impact speed and when cold, so the real peak force can be higher.

Which one to choose. An oil damper needs a long stroke, but it keeps the force low and stops the carriage with almost no bounce. A bumper saves stroke, but the force is higher for the same energy, and the carriage comes back. A bumper is a good choice when the energy is small: for example, when the drive limits the speed near the ends.

Catalog check. Catalogs rate a damper by energy per impact E = ½·m·v₀² + thrust × stroke, by the equivalent mass 2·E/v₀², and by the highest impact speed. Check also the return spring force, the body length, and the lowest equivalent mass the damper is made for.

Limits. One rigid mass. No friction in the guides, which would help a little. No field weakening: a drive that weakens the field can reach a higher speed. A pure c·vⁿ damper never brings the speed to zero. The last part of the energy goes into the hard stop at the end of the damper.
