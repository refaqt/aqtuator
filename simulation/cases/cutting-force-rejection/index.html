Power cannot be made unitless per newton: it grows with the square of the force amplitude. Twice the force gives four times the power. So the power chart uses the force amplitude set on the left. The mean force does no work in the steady state, because the stage does not move on average. This is the mechanical power only. For the ball screw it is the motor torque times the motor speed. The motor heat comes from the current, which follows the force (mean force included), and it is not in this chart.

How this is calculated

Linear motor. The stage is a single rigid mass: G(s) = 1/(m·s²), from force in N to position in m. The controller force is held constant over each sample (a zero-order hold). The discrete plant is G(z) = T²(z + 1) / (2m(z − 1)²), with T = 1/fs. This already holds the lag of the hold, about half a sample.

Ball-screw servo. A BLDC motor turns the ball screw through a coupling. The nut on the screw carries the stage. Everything is written in linear terms, with r = pitch/2π in m/rad. The motor torque τ becomes the force u = τ/r. This gives three masses in a chain: the rotor m1 = Jrotor/r², the screw ms = Jscrew/r² (steel, Jscrew = π·ρ·L·d⁴/32), and the stage m. The coupling joins m1 and ms with kc = coupling stiffness/r². The axial spring joins ms and the stage. It is three springs in series: 1/ka = 1/kshaft + 1/knut + 1/kbearing. The shaft part is kshaft = E·π·droot²/4 / xnut, with E = 206 GPa: with the fixed bearing at the motor end, only the shaft between the bearing and the nut carries the load. Each spring has a damper, c = 2ζ·√(k·μ), with μ the masses on either side of it. Each ζ is exact for its own mode when the other spring is rigid. The motor force acts on m1. The cutting force always acts on the stage. The controller reads the motor (the rotary encoder, collocated) or the stage (the linear encoder, non-collocated). The plant with its hold is found with one matrix exponential, so it is exact at the samples.

Controller. C(s) = K · 1/s · (s + 2πfi)(s + 2πfd) / (s + 2πflp)², with fi = fb/10, fd = fb/3 and flp = 4·fb. Each factor is translated to discrete time with the bilinear rule (Tustin), s = (2/T)(z − 1)/(z + 1). K is set so that |C·G| = 1 at fb. Each case gets its own K and fb, with the same rules. fb is the highest bandwidth where the loop meets all of these:

  • The sensitivity peak, the largest value of |1/(1 + C·G)|, is at most 6 dB (a factor 2; the limit is an input). So the loop never comes closer than 0.5 to the point −1, in any direction. This is the usual limit for motion systems. It also gives at least 6 dB gain margin and 29° phase margin.
  • The phase margin is at least 30° wherever |C·G| crosses 1. With a resonance, the loop can cross 1 again near the resonance.
  • The gain margin is at least 6 dB wherever the phase crosses −180° with |C·G| below 1.
  • The closed loop at the samples is stable.
  • All of this still holds when each resonance sits up to 15 % lower or higher than the model says (the spread is an input), with the same controller. The page tests 9 plants: coupling and axial stiffness each at (1 − 0.15)², 1 and (1 + 0.15)². The linear motor has no resonance, so it is one plant, with the same limits.

The search starts at a slow fb and stops at the first bandwidth where a rule breaks. A resonance that sits above 0 dB with a good phase at an even higher fb is not used: such a loop would be fragile.

Why fb follows the loop rate and not the mass. The controller shape is fixed, so the sensitivity peak of the continuous loop is the same at any bandwidth. The sampling lag adds to it, and that lag grows with fb/fs. So for the linear motor the 6 dB limit is reached at about fb = fs/35. A heavier stage only needs a larger K. The stiffness near fb is then about m·(2πfb)², so the mass and the loop rate both help. For the ball screw the resonances lower fb much further.

Cutting force. Fd(t) = Fmean + Famp · sin(2π ftooth t), with ftooth = teeth · rev/min / 60. It acts on the stage. The compliance is the stage position per newton of cutting force, and its inverse is the stiffness.

Vibration. The amplitude comes from a time simulation, not from the transfer function. The plant moves in continuous time, and it is integrated exactly between samples. The controller runs once per sample. The amplitude is half of the peak-to-peak motion of the stage after the start transient, including the motion between samples. Below about fs/4 this agrees with compliance × Famp within a few per cent. Above half the loop rate, the discrete transfer functions have no meaning, but the simulation still holds.

Controller force and power. The controller force u is the output of the controller after the hold. The force ratio is the amplitude of u over Famp in the steady state: about 1 well below fb, where the controller takes the whole force, a peak near fb, and falling above it, where the mass takes the force. The power is u·v, with v the speed where u pushes: the stage for the linear motor, the motor for the ball screw. Its mean is exact, because u is constant over each sample, so each sample adds u·Δx of work.

Limits.

  • Linear motor: one rigid mass. No resonances, no friction, no guide stiffness.
  • Ball screw: the screw does not twist, and the screw inertia sits in one point. No backlash, no friction, no guide or table stiffness.
  • The nut stiffness is the catalogue value at 30 % of the load rating. A nut without preload is softer at the small forces of this page, and it can lose contact when the force changes sign. A preloaded nut is stiffer.
  • The force is one pure sine plus a mean. A real cut has harmonics of the tooth frequency.
  • No sensor noise, no quantisation, no force or torque limit on the motor.
  • The cut does not react to the motion, so this model shows forced vibration, not chatter.
